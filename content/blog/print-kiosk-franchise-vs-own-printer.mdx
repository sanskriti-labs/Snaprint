If you already run a xerox or stationery shop and you're looking at self-service print options, you'll run into two fundamentally different approaches: add dedicated kiosk hardware alongside your existing setup, or keep using the printer you already own and add a software layer on top of it. Here's a factual look at what actually differs between the two, so you're deciding on real trade-offs rather than a sales pitch.

## The two models, plainly

**Dedicated kiosk hardware** means a separate machine — its own printer, its own touch display, its own paper tray — sits in your shop next to your existing counter setup. Customers interact with the kiosk directly; it's a self-contained unit built for one job.

**Software on your existing printer** means you keep the printer you already own and add an ordering layer — a QR code, an app, a web form — that routes customer orders to your existing hardware. No new machine, but your existing printer now has to handle both counter jobs and self-service jobs.

## What changes with dedicated hardware

- **Upfront cost is higher** — you're paying for a full machine, not just software.
- **Your existing printer stays free** for counter service and staff-run jobs; the kiosk absorbs the self-service volume separately.
- **Speed and capacity are built for volume** — dedicated hardware is typically sized for constant self-service use (large paper trays, sustained print speed), not shop-counter-level occasional bursts.
- **It's a second point of failure** — but also a second point of *capacity*. If the kiosk is down, your existing counter still works, and vice versa.
- **Physical footprint** — you need space in your shop for an additional unit.

## What changes with your own printer plus software

- **Lower upfront cost** — no new hardware, just an ordering layer.
- **Your printer does double duty** — every self-service order competes with counter jobs for the same machine's time and paper tray. During a rush, this can mean the queue you were trying to eliminate just moves from your counter to your printer.
- **Wear and volume matter** — a printer bought for shop-counter volume may not be rated for the sustained duty cycle of constant self-service use; check your printer's rated monthly duty cycle before assuming it can absorb both.
- **Faster to start** — no site visit or installation timeline for new hardware, since you're layering software onto what already exists.
- **Single point of failure** — if your one printer goes down, both your counter and your self-service channel go down together.

## The real decision factor: how much spare capacity your existing printer has

This isn't really a hardware-vs-software question — it's a capacity question. If your existing printer is already running near its practical daily limit serving counter customers, adding self-service orders on top of it doesn't solve your queue problem, it just relocates it. Dedicated hardware exists specifically to add capacity, not just add a new way to place an order.

If your shop has genuinely light, spread-out volume and your existing printer has real slack in its day, a software-only layer can make sense as a lower-cost way to test self-service demand before committing to new hardware.

## Who should choose which

- **Choose dedicated hardware** if your shop already has meaningful daily footfall and your existing printer is busy enough that adding more orders to it would just create a new bottleneck.
- **Consider a software-only layer** if your volume is light, spread out, and your current printer clearly has spare capacity most of the day.

Either way, the goal is the same: letting customers order without a staff member handling every step, without creating a new queue in a different place.
